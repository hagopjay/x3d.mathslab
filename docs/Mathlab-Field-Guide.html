01 / A useful first fifteen minutes

You do not need the theorem
before you can ask the question.

The first job of Math Lab is to make a mathematical relationship available to your eyes and hands. The second is to help you explain what you saw. The third is to make that explanation inspectable enough to improve.

This first extension begins with the supplied Manim MCP source and planning archive. That archive provides an animation-service foundation; it did not already contain this interactive X3D application. Its accounts of prior renders, installed software and production readiness describe another session. The delivered validation record, rather than those handoff claims, owns the evidence for this edition.

  1. Begin with one visible change.Open the linear-transformation lab. Change one matrix entry. Predict whether the square should stretch, shear, turn over or collapse before moving the control again.
  2. Read one measured quantity.Use the determinant, derivative error, integral error or optimization factor as a clue. A number is more useful when you know what it compares and which units it has.
  3. Reveal one deeper layer.Move from the visual question to the formula, then to its numerical check. You can learn the intuition without opening source, and you can inspect source without pretending the picture proves the theorem.
  4. Keep one experiment.Retain a lesson card with its parameters and export the X3D scene. Those two artifacts preserve different things: the card describes an experiment; the scene preserves a generated spatial representation.
The learning rhythm: predict → manipulate → observe → explain → check → revise. A useful surprise changes your next prediction. A decorative surprise merely changes the screen.

Choose your depth without changing your dignity.

CURIOUS

What would happen if…?

Use a single control and describe the shape of the change. “The area becomes zero” is a powerful observation before you know the word singular.

ADVANCED

Which statement is actually supported?

Separate a mathematical identity, an analytic bound, a sampled residual and a renderer observation. Then seek the input that would make the wrong implementation fail.

Use a reset when you lose the thread. Keep the previous parameter set when you find an interesting mistake. Work in pairs if possible: one person predicts, the other changes the control. Explaining a disagreement often teaches more than silently moving twenty sliders.

02 / A connected curriculum

Six rooms.
Many doors between them.

The lessons deliberately share ideas. A derivative is a local linear approximation. A gradient descent step is a transformation of an error vector. A Fourier approximation turns many simple rotations into a complicated signal. An ODE integrator turns local derivatives into a sampled path. None is an isolated visual trick.

The picture is a question generator. The model and reference tell you which answers it can support.

Four recurring questions

What is exact? A determinant or derivative formula may be evaluated from a known analytic expression. What is approximated? A curve is sampled, a sum is truncated, or an ODE is stepped. What is only displayed? Colors, line widths, camera position and projection. What is preserved? Parameters, generated coordinates and evidence identities need explicit owners.

03 / The room that changes the room

A matrix is a rule
for moving a basis.

In the linear lab, the columns of a two-by-two matrix describe where the two standard basis vectors go. Every input vector is a weighted combination of those basis vectors, so linearity tells you where every point goes. The transformed grid is the same rule repeated, not a separate animation effect.

A = [[a, b], [c, d]]
A(x, y) = (ax + by, cx + dy)
B(s) = (1 − s)I + sA,  0 ≤ s ≤ 1

The blend control moves from the identity to the chosen matrix along the straight path B(s) in matrix space. At intermediate values, inspect B(s), not just the final A. A perfectly invertible final matrix can have a singular intermediate blend. For example, blending I toward −I passes through the zero matrix at s = ½.

Area is not the same as shape.

The determinant ad − bc is the signed area scale of A. Its absolute value tells you how much an area changes; its sign distinguishes orientation preservation from reversal. A shear can preserve area while changing angles. A rotation can preserve both lengths and angles. A singular map collapses at least one independent direction, so the transformed square has zero area.

Go deeper · determinant and eigenvectors

The characteristic polynomial is λ² − tr(A)λ + det(A). Its discriminant tr(A)² − 4det(A) distinguishes two real eigenvalues, a repeated real value, or a complex-conjugate pair for a real two-by-two matrix. A real eigenvector keeps its line under the transformation: Av = λv. It need not stay in place. A negative λ reverses direction, zero collapses the vector, and λ = 1 leaves that particular vector unchanged.

A real rotation through an angle other than a multiple of π has no real eigenvector direction. That absence is an instructive result, not a drawing failure. Near repeated eigenvalues, finite-precision classification and vector construction require care; do not read many displayed decimals as a guarantee of a well-conditioned eigenbasis.

EXPERIMENT / PRESERVE AREA

Build a shear.

Try A = [[1, 1], [0, 1]]. Predict the determinant before observing the square. Then compare its area, perimeter and right angles. One invariant does not imply all geometric properties are preserved.

EXPERIMENT / REMOVE A DIMENSION

Make two columns dependent.

Try A = [[1, 2], [½, 1]]. The second column is twice the first. Explain why the entire plane maps onto one line, rather than only the unit square becoming unusually thin.

Practical heuristic: teach a transformation with three simultaneous witnesses: the moving basis, one ordinary vector and the image of a unit cell. The basis explains the rule; the vector tests your prediction; the cell reveals area and orientation.

Start values are a = 1.2, b = 0.6, c = 0.2, d = 0.9 and full blend. They are an authored example, not a special matrix. A later challenge system should retain its matrix, blend, expected property and tolerance together.

04 / Change and accumulation

A tangent asks “here.”
An integral asks “how much.”

Derivative: keep the location and the step distinct.

The derivative lesson uses a known smooth function so every numerical estimate has an analytic comparison. Moving x changes where you ask the question. Changing h changes how you approximate its answer. Those are different experiments.

f(x) = sin(x) + 0.15x²
f′(x) = cos(x) + 0.3x
Dforward = [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h
Dcentral = [f(x+h) − f(x−h)] / (2h)

The forward secant uses one interval to the right. The central estimate balances an interval on either side. For sufficiently smooth functions and moderate h, the forward truncation error is generally first order in h and the central error second order. The lab's quadratic term is differentiated exactly by the central formula; the remaining third derivative has magnitude at most one. That gives the central truncation bound h²/6 for this function.

Go deeper · why “smaller” eventually stops meaning “better”

Taylor expansions around x give f(x±h) = f(x) ± hf′(x) + h²f″(x)/2 ± h³f‴(ξ)/6. Subtracting the two expansions cancels the even-order term. Dividing by 2h leaves the derivative plus an O(h²) remainder. Floating-point subtraction introduces another error source: nearby function values can lose significant digits, and division by h amplifies the effect. A rough model combines a truncation term Ch² with a roundoff term proportional to ε/h.

The implementation deliberately exposes a bounded educational range of h; it is not a full arbitrary-precision roundoff laboratory. Even where only truncation dominates, distinguish the actual observed error from an upper bound. The bound can be valid without being tight.

Try: keep x = 0.7 fixed and reduce h from its initial 0.6. Predict which estimate should improve faster. Then move x while keeping h fixed. Find a location where the forward approximation happens to look unusually good. A locally favorable cancellation does not upgrade a method's general order.

Integral: the reference comes from an antiderivative.

The integral lab uses a positive curve on its lesson domain, which makes the rectangle picture immediately readable. Left, midpoint and right samples are different policies for representing each subinterval. Increase the rectangle count while keeping the interval and policy fixed before comparing policies.

g(x) = 0.25x² + 0.35cos(2x) + 1.2
G(x) = x³/12 + 0.175sin(2x) + 1.2x
∫abg(x) dx = G(b) − G(a)
Qn = Δx Σ g(a + (j+θ)Δx),  j = 0,…,n−1

Here θ is 0 for left endpoints, ½ for midpoints and 1 for right endpoints. The implementation's sample selector represents those choices with −1, 0 and +1. The initial interval is [−2, 2], with twelve midpoint rectangles. Inspect the reported difference from G(b) − G(a), not only the apparent fit of the rectangles.

Go deeper · a useful error bar with an explicit domain

For this integrand, g″(x) = 0.5 − 1.4cos(2x), so |g″(x)| ≤ 1.9. The composite midpoint error on a partition into n equal cells is bounded by 1.9|b−a|³/(24n²). The statement concerns a fixed smooth function, a specified interval and the midpoint rule. It is not a bound for arbitrary imported formulas or all quadrature schemes.

Rectangles are geometry; the quadrature sum is a number. Numerical accumulation should use the stated sample policy and width, not infer the number by measuring rendered pixels. When teaching signed integrals later, preserve the difference between oriented accumulation and unsigned geometric area; a positive first example is a teaching choice, not a definition of every integral.

Practical heuristic: whenever an approximation and exact reference coexist, show both plus their difference. Label a theoretical error bound separately. A row called “error” should never switch silently between those meanings.

The derivative and integral are connected by the fundamental theorem of calculus under suitable regularity. In this prototype, each lesson uses its own explicit function to keep its checks legible. A future linked lesson should show one chosen function, its derivative and its accumulated area from one shared definition.

05 / Many simple motions

A complicated signal
can begin with a circle.

The Fourier lesson connects rotating vectors to an odd-harmonic approximation of a square wave. Each added harmonic has a frequency, amplitude and phase contribution. The resulting sum is more than a decorative trail: it is a finite mathematical object that can be inspected at a chosen phase.

SN(t) = (4/π) Σk=0N−1 sin((2k+1)t)/(2k+1)

Begin with one term. Follow its rotation and its vertical contribution. Add the next odd harmonic and predict where it should reinforce or oppose the first. The initial five-term example and phase 0.9 are convenient starting points. They do not privilege five terms as a sufficient description of every signal.

Ask which meaning of “closer” you intend.

A finite trigonometric sum is continuous. It cannot reproduce a discontinuous jump everywhere without a transition region. More terms sharpen the transition, while ringing remains near the jump. The Gibbs phenomenon describes this persistent relative overshoot as the region containing it narrows; it does not mean the approximation fails to improve in every useful sense. The MIT notes in the references give a careful square-wave treatment.

Go deeper · the RMS tail is a different statement from pointwise error

For the unit-amplitude odd square wave, orthogonality of the sine modes gives its mean-square energy as one. The energy captured by these N odd harmonics is (8/π²)Σk=0N−11/(2k+1)². The theoretical RMS tail is therefore √[1 − (8/π²)Σ1/(2k+1)²]. This is an analytic consequence of the chosen target and basis. It is not the maximum visible error, the error at the current phase, or a measurement extracted from the plotted samples.

At a jump, Fourier convergence uses the average of the two one-sided limits under the usual conditions. Avoid assigning a single arbitrary value at the discontinuity and treating disagreement with that value as a failed computation. The finite sum has no literal vertical jump.

EXPERIMENT / TWO ERROR LENSES

Improve globally, ring locally.

Increase the term count. Compare the RMS tail with the behavior close to a discontinuity. Explain why one can improve while a narrow overshoot remains conspicuous.

EXPERIMENT / KEEP TIME HONEST

Move phase, not the camera.

Advance phase while keeping the sum fixed. Then add a harmonic while keeping phase fixed. Separating those operations makes frequency composition easier to understand.

Practical heuristic: a sampling density is part of a plotted signal's meaning. Before claiming the line resolves the highest harmonic, compare its spacing with that harmonic's period. The camera can make an under-sampled curve look smoother without adding information.
06 / Local rules, evolving consequences

When a path is the answer,
the step matters.

Lorenz: two kinds of separation.

The Lorenz system turns a three-component local rate rule into a trajectory. The familiar two-lobed picture is an invitation to investigate a dynamical system, not a proof that every chosen parameter set is chaotic.

dx/dt = σ(y−x)
dy/dt = x(ρ−z)−y
dz/dt = xy−βz

The default parameters are σ = 10, ρ = 28 and β = 8/3. The initial integration step is 0.01 over duration 20, and a nearby start is separated by 0.001. The prototype uses classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta. Its actual step is adjusted downward so the requested end time is reached exactly. Display coordinates map mathematical (x,y,z) to (0.36x, 0.36(z−23), 0.36y), putting the Lorenz z coordinate on visual height. Metrics retain the mathematical state units. It is a bounded finite experiment, not an infinite-time attractor computation.

Go deeper · RK4 order does not abolish chaos

For a sufficiently smooth problem on a fixed finite interval, classical RK4 has local truncation error O(h⁵) and global error O(h⁴) in its asymptotic regime. Error constants can grow strongly with time and the flow. Comparing h with h/2 is a useful numerical diagnostic, but the difference is not an exact-solution certificate and should not be conflated with the nearby-start distance.

The vector field's divergence is −(σ+1+β). For positive σ and β it implies local phase-volume contraction. Volume contraction alone does not establish a strange attractor or sensitive dependence for all initial conditions and parameters. A negative divergence is one property of the flow, not a general chaos detector.

Try: keep the duration short while refining the step. Then extend the duration. Compare a nearby-start experiment separately. A long-time mismatch may contain both amplified numerical error and genuine initial-state sensitivity; a visually dramatic divergence does not identify their proportions.

Gradient descent: the next step is a linear transformation of error.

The optimization lab uses a rotated positive-definite quadratic bowl. Rotation makes its principal directions less aligned with the screen; conditioning makes one direction steeper than the other.

u = cos(0.4)x + sin(0.4)y,  v = −sin(0.4)x + cos(0.4)y
F(x,y) = ½(u² + κv²),  κ ≥ 1
pj+1 = pj − α∇F(pj)
ej+1 = (I − αH)ej

The two eigenmode factors are 1−α and 1−ακ. A step that is gentle along the shallow direction may overshoot across the steep direction. The largest absolute factor, max(|1−α|, |1−ακ|), gives a precise contraction test for this quadratic. Uniform convergence for every starting point occurs when 0 < α < 2/κ. At the boundary, a mode can oscillate without shrinking; beyond it, a component in an unstable mode grows.

Go deeper · diagnose the algorithm in its eigenbasis

A symmetric positive-definite Hessian has an orthonormal eigenbasis. Writing the error vector in that basis turns the iteration into independent scalar recurrences ci,j+1 = (1−αλi)ci,j. This explains both the convergence interval and the alternating zigzag when one factor is negative. A special start with zero component in an unstable direction can hide instability; a test of one trajectory is weaker than the all-starts spectral condition.

The default κ = 8 and α = 0.15 lie within 0 < α < 0.25. A larger condition number narrows the permissible fixed-step interval and makes a single step size a more awkward compromise. This exact example should be understood before generalizing to nonquadratic loss surfaces, noisy gradients, momentum or adaptive optimizers.

The initial point is (2.2, 1.6), with 28 iterations. Surface height is multiplied by 0.6/√κ for viewing; reported losses retain their original mathematical scale. Unstable paths can leave the displayed surface domain. The numerical result must report that condition rather than drawing a clipped path as if it had stopped or converged. Omitted geometry is a display budget; it is not a successful termination criterion.

Practical heuristic: demonstrate a method beside a case it should visibly fail. A stable quadratic step, an oscillatory boundary step and an unstable step teach more together than three attractive convergent runs.
07 / The spatial model has a life outside the page

Export the mathematical object,
not only its photograph.

X3D gives the lab a structured spatial representation with named objects, coordinates, materials, cameras and standard scene-graph nodes. A line can remain a line, a surface can retain its vertices and faces, and a generated scene can be inspected by another consumer. The value is concrete: the browser is no longer the only place where the spatial result exists.

The prototype's Python kernel owns the formulas, normalized parameters, sampled geometry and numerical metrics. The X3D adapter turns supported primitives into standard scene nodes. The browser controls presentation and interaction. The Manim path turns a retained sample into another explanatory medium. This division prevents a caption or a camera effect from becoming the hidden source of a mathematical answer.

Native behavior can grow where it helps.

X3D's event model includes declarative ROUTE connections; TimeSensor and interpolators can express useful timed scene changes. A future animation export could bake a sampled path into standard nodes and make that portion portable without the surrounding web app. That is distinct from embedding arbitrary executable logic or assuming a browser clock is a fixed numerical integration clock. The present static export should be read according to its adapter contract.

Practical heuristic: keep mathematical coordinates separate from camera and display transforms. A pretty scale change must not alter a derivative, distance, integration step or convergence diagnosis. Record any intentional display normalization so another consumer can interpret the scene.
Go deeper · decimal digits are not a precision contract

The current adapter uses standard Coordinate nodes, whose point field is MFVec3f. The mathematical kernel computes in Python floating-point arithmetic, while this representation introduces a single-precision coordinate boundary. Writing many decimal digits into XML does not make that field double precision. Metrics should continue to compare the mathematical quantities in their declared numerical domain rather than measure the rendered line.

Large offsets, very thin geometry and nearly coincident curves can expose this distinction. A later high-dynamic-range lesson should budget coordinate error, consider origin rebasing or an appropriate higher-precision representation, and verify the actual consumer. File bytes, parsed values and GPU buffers are related identities, not automatically identical precision domains.

When WebGL is unavailable, a clearly named two-dimensional projection can keep the learning controls usable. That fallback is another view of the sampled data. It is not evidence that native three-dimensional rendering succeeded, nor does it reveal depth in the same way as an orbitable X3D view.

08 / Rehearse the explanation

A lab invites a question.
A film can pace its answer.

The supplied Manim server contributes an important second rhythm. Interactive exploration lets the learner choose the next change. A scripted explanation can choose a useful sequence: isolate one basis vector, reveal a second one, transform the grid, then introduce the determinant. Neither medium replaces the other.

The bridge's central design choice is to reuse the shared numerical sample rather than silently recompute a different problem inside a decorative animation. A baked scene can trace the same coordinates and present the same retained metrics. That preserves a practical correspondence, while allowing explicit differences in projection, styling, timing and available geometry.

What was worth retaining from the archive

Structured render results, actionable diagnostics, preview sidecars, quality selection and content identities support an iterative explanation workflow. A failed render should explain whether it failed to import a dependency, typeset a label, find a scene or finish within its limit. A successful process exit should still be checked for the expected artifact.

The current source audit strengthens several service boundaries: artifact IDs and paths, bounded configuration, shared concurrency, duplicate-job ownership, timeout/cancellation cleanup, nonblocking preview work and cache publication/identity. Its tests use explicit registration/process adapters and fake media payloads; those controls should not be described as successful Manim films. The separate Manim audit and release record explain the exact scope, including any later actual rendering.

The archive's heuristic quality estimates are planning hints, not measured completion-time guarantees. Cache identities need their input domain stated; the same Python text and scene name under a different engine need not render identically. A template or generated source that compiles is also distinct from a video that was actually rendered and observed.

A crucial trust boundary: rendering Manim Python executes Python. A scene-name check, syntax validator or subprocess argument list does not make arbitrary scene code a sandbox. The interactive Math Lab evaluates known bounded lesson parameters; it should not expose an arbitrary-Python render endpoint as though it were the same operation. Use the optional rendering path only in its declared local trust context.

Compose a useful explanation in four beats.

  1. State the question.“Why does increasing this rate make a downhill algorithm worse?” Keep the opening diagram simple enough to predict.
  2. Show the mechanism.Draw the gradient direction and one explicit update. Move only what the current sentence explains.
  3. Reveal the comparison.Place a stable and unstable setting beside each other, naming which parameter changed. Retain a fixed camera and scale unless the scale change itself is explained.
  4. Return agency.End with a parameter choice or challenge the learner can try in the interactive lab. A film can make a question more interesting without pretending to settle every extension.

No model key is needed to explore the local lessons or generate their declared outputs. Optional Manim rendering still needs its own installed dependencies and execution environment. Consult the delivered validation and Manim audit for the exact rendered, tested or not-run scope of this edition.

09 / Know what the check checked

A green result needs
a noun after it.

“Validated” is incomplete until you identify the object and criterion. XML can parse while a mathematical mesh is wrong. An analytic residual can be small while an interactive control applies a stale response. A renderer can display a scene faithfully while the scene encodes the wrong function.

MATHEMATICAL CHECK

Compare with an independent relation.

Use the analytic derivative, antiderivative, determinant identity or exact quadratic amplification. Avoid a test that merely calls the same mistaken helper twice.

REPRESENTATION CHECK

Inspect the consumed object.

Check coordinate finiteness, index ranges, units, metadata escaping and deterministic output. A versioned card, a numerical sample and a scene need their own identities.

INTERACTION CHECK

Make an old answer arrive late.

Changing lesson or parameters can leave an earlier request in flight. Only the current response should own the controls, metrics and downloadable scene. Export should not combine a new title with old geometry.

LEARNING CHECK

Ask a person for a prediction.

A numerical test cannot establish comprehension. Ask what should happen next and why, then watch whether the learner can transfer the idea to a different example.

Failure cases are teaching assets.

Keep a singular matrix, a deliberately coarse quadrature, a Fourier transition, a long-time Lorenz comparison and an unstable gradient step. Each has a healthy counterpart and a declared meaning. The point is not to make every screen green; it is to make a correct diagnosis visible.

Input validation should reject unknown lessons, unknown parameter keys, nonfinite numbers, unsupported types and requests outside their declared budgets. Refusal should preserve the last valid experiment. A bound on input parameters is only the first gate: expanded points, faces, scene nodes, generated source size and response work also need limits.

Practical heuristic: a report should retain source identity, normalized parameters, algorithm settings and the exact comparison it performed. Preserve previous records as historical; do not relabel an older renderer or host result as fresh after changing the application.

The repository's final validation record owns executable counts and direct browser observations. This guide explains the concepts and design. It does not mint a formal Loom receipt, cross-host bitwise guarantee, hardware-performance claim or general mathematical theorem from an attractive demonstration.

10 / A broad vision with useful increments

Build a mathematical world
that can explain itself.

The long-term opportunity is an environment where one question can move between a diagram, a numerical experiment, a symbolic explanation, a spatial scene and a paced film without losing its identity. The most valuable expansion makes those relationships clearer, rather than merely adding another gallery of unrelated animations.

Three architectural investments pay repeatedly.

A small mathematical intermediate model. Keep lesson definitions, parameters, primitives, metrics and provenance explicit. It becomes a meeting place for native X3D, Manim, accessible tables and future renderers. Avoid turning it prematurely into a general symbolic system or a disguised programming language.

An evidence vocabulary. A value can be analytic, approximated, bounded, sampled, observed or conjectured. Those categories should survive into captions and exports. A future assistant could explain a surprising graph more usefully if it knows which quantities are exact and which depend on integration step or display clipping.

Pedagogical composition. A lesson graph should connect prerequisite ideas and useful counterexamples. For example: basis → matrix → eigenmode → gradient stability; local derivative → ODE step → accumulated numerical error → sensitivity. Such connections give the learner a route back when an advanced object becomes confusing.

Keep ambitious features accountable.

Symbolic tools can supply identities and derivations, but their output needs assumptions and a declared verification path. Research ingestion can suggest a lesson, but it does not turn a conjecture into a theorem. A captured scene can support reproducibility, but a complete interactive session also needs application state and versioned rules. Shared classroom sessions need clear authority and accessibility before they need a large social layer.

The best future Math Lab does not merely answer “what does this look like?” It helps a learner ask “what would convince me that I understand it?”
